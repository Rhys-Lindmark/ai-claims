## Provisional three-claim denominator

### Free to Choose

1. Voluntary exchange and competitive choice generally coordinate decentralized information better than detailed government allocation.
2. Sustained inflation ultimately requires excessive growth of nominal money or government liabilities relative to real output, but a constant money-growth rule is superior to discretionary interest-rate policy.
3. Education vouchers generally improve student outcomes by giving families choice and schools competitive incentives.

### Why Liberalism Failed

1. Liberalism's emphasis on individual autonomy weakens inherited community, association, family, and place-based obligations.
2. Liberal market and state institutions jointly produce both greater formal freedom and increasingly centralized public and private power.
3. Liberalism has failed on its own terms across liberal democracies rather than producing historically large gains in rights, welfare, longevity, education, and choice.

### The Spirit Level

1. Among rich societies, higher income inequality is associated with worse population health and a broader bundle of social problems.
2. Status competition, chronic stress, and weaker trust mediate an independent causal effect of inequality beyond absolute deprivation.
3. Inequality alone is the dominant general explanation for the book's cross-national and U.S. state-level outcome patterns.

### The Entrepreneurial State

1. Public agencies have financed foundational technologies and basic or mission-oriented research that private investors underprovide.
2. Public research funding can causally stimulate complementary private innovation rather than merely displacing it.
3. The state is generally better positioned than decentralized firms and investors to choose and develop the technological directions that drive long-run growth.

### The Great Leveler

1. Mass-mobilization war, transformative revolution, state collapse, and severe pandemics have repeatedly reduced income or wealth inequality.
2. Violent shocks level through capital destruction, mortality, labor scarcity, expropriation, taxation, and institutional change.
3. Large and durable reductions in inequality have historically occurred only through these violent “Four Horsemen.”

### The Rise and Decline of Nations

1. Stable societies accumulate narrow distributional coalitions that seek concentrated benefits while diffusing costs.
2. These coalitions slow decision-making, reduce adaptation, and lower economic growth.
3. Postwar growth differences across Britain, Germany, Japan, and other nations are explained mainly by how war disrupted accumulated coalitions.

### The Logic of Collective Action

1. Individuals can rationally free-ride when a group's public good is non-excludable and one contribution has little effect on provision.
2. Smaller groups organize more readily than larger latent groups because individual stakes and observability are greater.
3. Large groups usually require selective incentives, coercion, or organizational entrepreneurs to provide collective goods.

### Capitalism, Socialism and Democracy

1. Capitalism evolves through creative destruction in which innovation replaces firms, products, skills, and organizational forms.
2. Large firms and temporary market power can sometimes finance and organize innovation rather than perfect competition always maximizing progress.
3. Capitalism's success inevitably generates intellectual, bureaucratic, and political forces that transform it into socialism.

### Development as Freedom

1. Development should be measured by people's substantive capabilities and agency, not income growth alone.
2. Political freedoms, social opportunities, transparency, and economic facilities reinforce one another as both means and ends of development.
3. Functioning multiparty democracies with a free press prevent catastrophic famine by creating accountability and information pressure.

### The Price of Inequality

1. High inequality weakens economic mobility and causes unequal opportunity to persist across generations and places.
2. Concentrated wealth increases political influence and helps shape rules that preserve rents and further inequality.
3. Reducing inequality through well-designed taxes, transfers, education, competition, and public investment can improve rather than necessarily reduce durable growth.

## Review constraints

- Separate philosophical or normative arguments from falsifiable causal claims.
- Do not treat “market,” “state,” “liberalism,” “inequality,” “innovation,” “group,” or “development” as unitary interventions.
- Distinguish association, mechanism evidence, historical case fit, and identified causal effects.
- For public R&D, separate basic research, mission funding, procurement, demonstration, late-stage commercialization, and firm execution.
- For inequality, separate income from wealth, within-country from global inequality, relative from absolute mobility, and poverty from distribution.
- For collective action, preserve group size, individual return, observability, communication, repetition, identity, and selective incentives as separate variables.
- Use exactly three claims and disclose their denominator rather than scoring an entire ideology or worldview.
