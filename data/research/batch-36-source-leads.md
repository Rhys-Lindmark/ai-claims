## Provisional three-claim denominator

### Thinking in Bets

1. A decision's quality should be judged from the information and process available at the time, not the realized outcome alone.
2. Explicit probabilities and decision records reduce hindsight distortion and improve learning from uncertain results.
3. Truth-seeking groups with norms of dissent and accuracy reliably improve individual decisions.

### Superforecasting

1. A small subset of forecasters repeatedly outperforms average participants on resolvable geopolitical questions.
2. Decomposition, base rates, frequent updating, calibration feedback, and team discussion improve probabilistic forecasts.
3. These methods generalize from short-horizon tournament questions to most consequential long-range policy and tail-risk problems.

### Noise

1. Professionals making nominally identical judgments show substantial unwanted variability across judges and occasions.
2. Aggregation, structured rules, independent judgments, and decision hygiene can reduce error from noise.
3. Reducing variance is generally beneficial even where justified discretion, heterogeneity, adaptation, or correlated error matters.

### The Signal and the Noise

1. Probabilistic forecasts should be judged by calibration and proper scores across repeated events rather than by isolated wins or losses.
2. Bayesian updating, base rates, model comparison, and domain knowledge improve prediction in data-rich recurring domains.
3. The same forecasting framework transfers cleanly across elections, weather, economics, earthquakes, epidemics, climate, poker, and terrorism.

### The Black Swan

1. In fat-tailed domains, rare observations can dominate totals and make sample means, variance estimates, and point forecasts unstable.
2. People and institutions systematically underestimate model error, unseen alternatives, and extreme events.
3. Important historical and market events are generally unforeseeable Black Swans rather than events whose probability or mechanisms can be usefully modeled.

### Fooled by Randomness

1. Survivorship selection and multiple testing make some unskilled performers appear persistently exceptional by chance.
2. Evaluators systematically mistake lucky outcomes for skill and construct causal stories after results are known.
3. Apparent success in financial markets is generally dominated by luck rather than persistent, measurable skill.

### The Paradox of Choice

1. Larger choice sets can reduce selection, satisfaction, or confidence when decisions are complex and preferences uncertain.
2. Maximizing tendencies can increase comparison, regret, and dissatisfaction relative to satisficing.
3. More choice generally reduces wellbeing across consumer, career, relationship, and identity domains.

### Scarcity

1. Resource scarcity captures attention and creates short-term focus benefits on the urgent problem.
2. Scarcity also taxes cognitive bandwidth, increases tunneling, and can worsen longer-run decisions.
3. One general scarcity mechanism explains similar cognitive effects of poverty, time pressure, dieting, loneliness, and organizational overload.

### Behave

1. Human behavior emerges from interacting neural, hormonal, developmental, social, cultural, ecological, and evolutionary causes across timescales.
2. Hormones such as testosterone modulate context-sensitive tendencies rather than directly causing aggression or dominance.
3. Biological explanation eliminates a scientifically meaningful role for agency or moral responsibility.

### The Scout Mindset

1. Motivated reasoning often recruits argument and evidence to defend identity or preferred conclusions.
2. Actively open-minded thinking, calibration, curiosity, and willingness to update are associated with more accurate judgment.
3. Teaching a scout mindset reliably transfers to consequential decisions and durable behavior across domains.

## Review constraints

- Separate forecast calibration, discrimination, resolution, scoring, and decision value.
- Do not generalize from short, resolvable tournament questions to long-horizon structural or fat-tail uncertainty without evidence.
- Distinguish bias from noise, justified heterogeneity, irreducible uncertainty, and measurement error.
- Define the tail, exposure, loss function, and model uncertainty before labeling an event a Black Swan.
- Preserve moderators in choice overload and scarcity rather than averaging them into universal effects.
- Do not infer philosophical conclusions about agency directly from correlates or precursors of neural choice.
- Use exactly three central claims and disclose the denominator.
